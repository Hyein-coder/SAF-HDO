## Findings

The common TEA convention is to represent HDO with **fixed stoichiometry, conversion, and product-yield assumptions**, often in Aspen `RYield`/`RStoic` blocks. Product cuts are then specified from measured fuel properties or distillation curves. A smaller set of studies use lumped kinetics in `RCSTR`/`RPlug` models; these can respond to some operating conditions, but their kinetics are catalyst- and feed-specific. I found no verified study that establishes a single catalyst-agnostic HDO model spanning sulfided and noble-metal catalysts.

### Sensitivity and modeling implications

- **Hydrogen consumption and carbon/fuel yield:** Strong cost drivers in pyrolysis upgrading. PNNL design cases and feedstock TEAs generally calculate sensitivity around fixed process yields or design assumptions. A yield change can affect MSP without predicting how temperature, pressure, or feed composition would cause that change.
- **Catalyst cost and life:** Liu & Wang examine both economically; PNNL’s 2020 analysis attributes a significant MFSP share to hydrotreating catalyst. Ma et al. explicitly model coking/deactivation, so its yield response has a kinetic basis, though a narrow one.
- **WHSV/LHSV, pressure, and temperature:** Design reports specify these operating conditions, but specified conditions alone do not make a yield model predictive. Ma et al. and Cordero-Lanzac et al. provide more predictive kinetic treatments; transfer across catalyst families is not demonstrated.
- **LCA/GHG:** The PNNL 2020 work identifies hydrogen-production natural gas and process energy as major GHG levers. Fixed-yield assumptions mean GHG responses to changed operating conditions can omit accompanying changes in product quality, carbon loss, and hydrogen demand.

### Methods to adopt

1. **Use a hierarchical lump model** for oxygenate families and pathways (hydrogenation, HDO, decarbonylation/decarboxylation, cracking, condensation/coke), with catalyst descriptors modifying pathway rates. Keep catalyst identity or descriptor effects explicit rather than claiming one universal parameter set.
2. **Embed kinetics in Aspen `RPlug`/`RCSTR` or an external Python model** called through a flowsheet interface. Track temperature, H₂ partial pressure, and residence time/LHSV, plus deactivation if the available data support it.
3. **Represent products with both chemical lumps and boiling cuts.** Calibrate aggregate elemental balance and H₂ uptake, then validate distillation curve and carbon-number distribution separately; do not infer one uniquely from the other.
4. **Fit parameters across experiments, not just one product distribution.** Use multi-condition data with mass/carbon/oxygen/hydrogen balance constraints and uncertainty intervals. Bayesian optimization can tune parameters, but requires observations across operating conditions to produce predictive response surfaces.
5. **Use GP/ANN/polynomial surrogates only after generating kinetic-model cases** over the intended operating envelope. Existing SAF MSP surrogates demonstrate economic interpolation, not reaction-model substitution.
